Why Do We Celebrate Halloween?

Ancient Origins

The Celtic Samhain Festival

Over 2,000 years ago, the Celts of Ireland, the UK, and northern France celebrated Samhain (pronounced sow-in) on October 31st. It marked the end of the harvest, the slaughtering of livestock for winter, and the start of the dark Celtic New Year.

The Spirit Veil

Thinning of the Boundary

Celts believed that on October 31st, the veil separating the physical realm from the afterlife dissolved. Spectral spirits, fairies (Aos Sí), and ancestral ghosts could freely cross over to roam among the living.

Roman Influence

Feralia & Pomona Harvests

When the Roman Empire conquered Celtic lands in the 1st century AD, they combined Samhain with Feralia (a Roman day commemorating the dead) and a festival honoring Pomona, goddess of fruit and orchards.

Christian Adaptation

All Hallows' Eve

In the 8th century, Pope Gregory III designated November 1st as "All Saints' Day" to honor Christian martyrs. The night before became known as All Hallows' Eve, which eventually shortened into Halloween.

All Souls' Day

Commemorating the Faithful

In 1000 AD, the Church established November 2nd as All Souls' Day—a day to pray for souls in purgatory. Bonfires, parades, and dressing up in saint and demon costumes remained popular customs.

Arrival in America

19th Century Immigrants

When massive waves of Irish and Scottish immigrants fled the Potato Famine in the 1840s, they brought Halloween lore to North America, blending European folk customs with native American autumn harvests.
